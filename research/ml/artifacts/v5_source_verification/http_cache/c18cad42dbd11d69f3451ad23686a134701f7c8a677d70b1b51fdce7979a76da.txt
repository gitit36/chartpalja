Mia Martini A voice that changed Italian song Mia Martini, pseudonym of Domenica Rita Adriana BertÃ¨, was one of the most intense and recognizable interpreters of Italian music. Born in Bagnara Calabra and raised in the Marche region, she is considered one of the most important voices of the 20th-century Italian music scene, capable of blending technique, emotion, and drama with a naturalness that made her interpretations unique. Her vocal range, flexibility across registers, and interpretative depth established her as one of the most beloved and studied figures of the "canzone dâautore." Albums like Oltre la collina and hits like Piccolo uomo , Minuetto , Donna , Gli uomini non cambiano , and Almeno tu nellâuniverso marked different eras of her career, providing a portrait of a free, consistent, and deeply human artist. Childhood and beginnings Origins and first steps Domenica Rita Adriana BertÃ¨ was born in Bagnara Calabra but spent much of her childhood in the Marche region, between Porto Recanati and Macerata, where her father, a Latin and Greek teacher, worked as a high school principal. Within the family, she was affectionately called "MimÃ¬" and soon manifested a very lively passion for music, listening to voices on the radio and imitating the great interpreters of the time. While still a teenager, she began performing in dance halls and small venues, participating in her first talent contests. In 1962, she convinced her mother to accompany her to Milan to audition for several record labels. It was the beginning of a difficult journey, made of apprenticeships, relocations, and changes of stage names, but also of a determination that would lead her, a few years later, to transform into Mia Martini: the voice that would give Italian song a new emotional dimension. She participated in the Pesaro Festival with Ombrello blu and, in 1963, recorded her first 45s for Juke Box. Her first success came in 1964 with a victory at the Bellaria Festival with the track Come puoi farlo tu , while Il magone offered her some television notoriety. Appearances on RAI programs and new singles followed, such as Ed ora che abbiamo litigato , presented on Studio Uno . Despite the good premises, her career struggled to take off. Demos collected for an album remained unreleased for decades, and attempts with Durium yielded no results. MimÃ¬ began to distance herself from the lighthearted persona created for her, drawn instead to soul and blues sounds closer to her nature. Having moved to Rome with her mother and sisters, she formed a trio with Loredana and their friend Renato Fiacchini (the future Renato Zero), trying to support herself with small jobs. In 1969, a painful episode marked her life: she was arrested in Sardinia for possession of a marijuana cigarette and spent four months in the Tempio Pausania prison. The charge proved unfounded and she was acquitted, but the experience left a deep mark, fueling her desire for artistic and personal rebirth. During the same period, she participated as a backing vocalist, alongside Loredana, in Chico Buarque de Hollanda's album Per un pugno di samba , and began performing live thanks to pianist Toto Torquati, who sensed her authentic talent. Success: Mia Martini The birth of a new identity The breakthrough came at the beginning of the 1970s with the decisive meeting with Alberigo Crocetta, founder of the Piper club and talent scout. It was he who reinvented her with the stage name "Mia Martini": "Mia" in homage to Mia Farrow, and "Martini" to evoke an Italy recognizable abroad. The young artist abandoned the lighthearted 1960s look for a more personal and bohemian style, made of rings, ethnic fabrics, and a black bowler hat that would become her visual signature. In 1971, she released the single Padre davvero... with RCA Italiana, written by Antonello De Sanctisâa track with bold lyrics about generational conflict. Despite censorship, the interpretation struck people with its strength and sincerity, winning the Viareggio Festival. This was the prelude to her first album, Oltre la collina , one of the first Italian concept albums, where she addressed existential themes such as religion, loneliness, and illness. Critics welcomed it as a disruptive work, and Lucio Battisti, struck by her voice "different from all the others," invited her to perform on his television special Tutti insieme . Simultaneously, Mia collaborated with a young Claudio Baglioni, who wrote GesÃ¹ Ã¨ mio fratello and Lacrime di marzo for her, tracks that consolidated her spiritual and dramatic style. Other titles like Credo and CosÃ¬ sia showed a new sensitivity in auteur songwriting, propelling her toward the artistic maturity that would soon explode with Piccolo uomo and Minuetto . Oltre la collina During the making of Oltre la collina , Mia Martini recounted that she poured into the record everything she had experienced up to that point: prison, travels, the distance from her father, and the rebellious years between Ibiza and Kathmandu. She said recording those songs had been like "setting an examination of conscience to music." That album, born of both pain and freedom, would forever mark her voice and her history. From "Piccolo uomo" to "Minuetto" (1972â1973) Consecration and the birth of the myth When Alberigo Crocetta left RCA for Ricordi, Mia Martini followed him, sensing that mutual trust could open new possibilities. It was in this phase that she recorded Piccolo uomo , written by Bruno Lauzi and Michelangelo La Bionda with music by Dario Baldan Bembo. The track, initially intended for the band I Camaleonti, was entrusted to Mia amidst general skepticism. Yet, her intense and soulful interpretation transformed what was meant to be a pop song into a declaration of artistic identity. Presented at the Festival Pop, Beat, Western Express in London and then at Festivalbar, Piccolo uomo won over the public and the competition, giving Mia Martini her first Gold Record and official entry into the elite of Italian music. In September of the same year, she appeared at the Venice International Exhibition of Light Music with Donna sola , a track with soul undertones that confirmed her dramatic and refined talent. The single sold over 270,000 copies, and in 1973, it earned her the Gondola d'Oro . In October, she published her second album, Nel mondo, una cosa âa sophisticated work that included tracks by VinÃ­cius de Moraes, Chico Buarque, and John Lennon, and which earned her the Record Critics' Award for best LP of the year. By early 1973, Mia's popularity extended beyond Italian borders: Germany, France, and Spain welcomed her as "the queen of young Italian music." On April 2, she recorded Minuetto , signed by Dario Baldan Bembo and Franco Califano. The lyrics came from a complex drafting process, rewritten several times until becoming an intimate and universal tale of love that holds and consumes. Califano was openly inspired by Mia's personal experiences, and Baldan Bembo built a symphonic arrangement that fused classical and pop elements. The result is one of the absolute masterpieces of Italian song: Minuetto remained in the top ten for twenty-two consecutive weeks, reached number one, and earned her a Gold Record, a Platinum Record, and her second consecutive Festivalbar victoryâa feat previously achieved only by Lucio Battisti. At the end of 1973, Mia Martini was the top-selling Italian female singer of the year, alongside Ornella Vanoni and Patty Pravo: a victory built not on hype or trends, but on talent and authenticity. The power of Minuetto During the recording of Minuetto , Dario Baldan Bembo recalled years later that Mia entered the studio "silent and focused, as if she knew she had something unrepeatable in her hands." after the first take, no one spoke: her voice, between sweetness and despair, had captured everyone. Califano simply said that the track no longer belonged to him, but to her. From that day, Minuetto ceased to be a song and became a destiny. The record label demanded the singer only record tracks from its internal catalog, reducing the freedom of choice Mia had always determinedly defended. Forced to publish a new LP to honor her contract, she recorded Un altro giorno con me , a record she perceived as imposed but managed to elevate with her interpretative power. Voice and freedom When she signed the new contract with RCA, Mia Martini confided to a friend that she finally felt "mistress of her own voice again." In reality, she knew well that this freedom had cost her everything: peace with the label, money, even her home. But she didn't hesitate for a second. "Better to lose everything than to no longer recognize myself in what I sing," she said. It was 1976, and that phrase became the symbol of her artistic courage. From triumphs at the Olympia to meeting Fossati (1977â1979) Between Paris and Genoa: The mature voice of Mia Martini In 1977, during a television concert in France, Mia Martini was noticed by Charles Aznavour. The great chansonnier was struck by her interpretative intensity and invited her to perform alongside him in a series of international recitals. The meeting with Aznavour marked the beginning of her European dimension: French elegance and Mediterranean drama merged into a voice now recognizable everywhere. That same year, she represented Italy at the Eurovision Song Contest with Libera . Also in 1977, she recorded Per amarti , which marked the decisive meeting with Ivano Fossati. The eponymous album included Ritratto di donna , with which she won the Most Outstanding Performance Award at the World Popular Song Festival in Tokyo. The following year, she debuted at the Olympia in Paris with Aznavour to great acclaim. Between 1978 and 1979, her relationship with Fossati intertwined with music. Their collaboration produced Danza , an album entirely written and produced by Fossati, considered one of the expressive peaks of Mia Martini's career. However, behind the musical perfection lay deep tensions. Lites with RCA pushed her to change labels to Warner Bros. In 1979, Danza was officially released, but the intense and tormented relationship with Fossati marked the start of a deep personal crisis. This phase ended with a vocal cord ailment that forced her into silence for nearly a year. The voice that went out After the release of Danza , Mia Martini told a friend she felt her "voice going out like a candle." It wasn't just fatigue: her vocal cords had stiffened, preventing her from singing. Doctors spoke of a very rare case. She replied calmly: "Then I will return to sing when silence is also rare." she succeeded, but the sound was never the sameâit became deeper, as if carrying the memory of that silence. Withdrawal from the spotlight (1983â1989) Silence and exile At the end of 1983, struck by the death of her road manager and friend Pier, the end of her relationship with Ivano Fossati, and a temporary detachment from her sister Loredana, Mia Martini decided to abandon the stage. The withdrawal was also a direct consequence of the infamous rumors that, since the 1970s, had labeled her a "jinx" (porta jella)âan absurd but devastating prejudice fueled by superstition in the entertainment world. For many years, those rumors closed doors, contracts, and broadcasts. In 1982, she publicly denounced the boycott she suffered: RAI no longer broadcast her tracks, and many colleagues refused to perform alongside her. Humiliated and professionally isolated, she chose to voluntarily disappear. she retired to Umbria, living in solitude and economic precariousness. However, she did not stop singing entirely: at the Teatro Ciak in Milan, she organized two event-concerts that became the album Miei compagni di viaggio (1983). The concert closed with Chico Buarque's Ed ora dico sul serio , with the emblematic line: "I would not like to sing anymore." In 1985, she attempted a return to Sanremo with Paolo Conte's Spaccami il cuore but was excluded from selection. After breaking with the DDD label, she was left without a contract and returned to performing in small theaters and squares, away from the spotlight. The years that followed were of marginalization but also research: a long silence that prepared the rebirth. The day she sang again Those close to her said that during the Umbrian period, Mia would turn on the record player every morning with John Lennon's Imagine and try to follow the melody under her breath, like a ritual. "After all, the voice is just a way not to be silent," she said. One day in 1988, during an evening with friends, she sang Piccolo uomo in its entirety again. At the end, no one applauded: they were all in tears. It was then she knew the silence was over.
